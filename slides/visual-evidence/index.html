The title is "Seeing Threat Unfold: Dynamic Visual Information Shapes Police Decisions to Shoot." The idea is simple: a shoot decision unfolds over time, and the visual information in the scene unfolds with it. I want to ask which of that information drives the officer's evidence accumulation, and whether it helps explain the race effect we see in police shooting decisions.

The GoDDM

Start point (A): no race difference. Drift (C): weaker toward don't shoot for Black suspects, only when unarmed and nonantagonistic.

SCRIPT: Now the behaviour. These four panels show how often officers shot, by suspect race, in each condition. [Click 1] The key panel is unarmed and nonantagonistic. Here, officers shot 22 percent of Black suspects but only 5 percent of White suspects. That is the race gap in errors. In the other panels it is not there: with antagonistic unarmed suspects the raw rates even reverse, and armed suspects are shot 96 to 98 percent of the time regardless of race. [Click 2] Here is a quick preview of the published model. On the left is the GoDDM: after a non-decision time, evidence starts at a start point and drifts toward the shoot boundary or away from it; the threshold is the distance between the boundaries, and a shoot happens when evidence reaches the upper one. In panel A, the start point, White and Black overlap, so race does not change where the decision starts. In panel C, the drift rate, officers accumulated weaker evidence toward "don't shoot" for Black suspects, and only when the suspect was unarmed and nonantagonistic. So the race effect lives in the evidence accumulation. That is exactly where visual information should enter, and that is what I test today. BACKGROUND: THE GoDDM (Pleskac, Cesario, Johnson & Gagnon, 2025; their Table 1 and Figure 1) What it is: the diffusion decision model (DDM; Ratcliff) describes a decision as noisy evidence accumulating over time until it reaches one of two thresholds. The clock starts when the object is drawn (object onset). It is fitted to each officer's trials: whether they shot, and when. Why "Go": officers make only one explicit response, shoot. Not shooting has no response time. Go/no-go work (Gomez et al., 2007; Ratcliff et al., 2018) shows people use the same accumulation process in go/no-go as in two-choice tasks; the no-go ("don't shoot") boundary is implicit. So a don't-shoot trial is treated as a hit on the lower boundary whose time is missing (censored); the model uses the probability of not shooting together with the shoot RTs. The four parameters: - Relative start point β (0 to 1): where evidence starts between the two thresholds, i.e. a bias before the object is seen. Above .5 = closer to shoot; below .5 = closer to don't shoot. - Threshold separation α (above 0): the distance between the shoot threshold (at α) and the don't-shoot threshold (at 0); it is caution. Larger = more evidence needed, so slower but more accurate decisions (the speed-accuracy tradeoff). - Drift rate δ (any value): the average strength of evidence per unit time. The sign is the direction (positive = toward shoot, negative = toward don't shoot); the size is the quality of the information. - Relative non-decision time NDT′ (0 to 1): the share of the response time, relative to the minimum observed response time, spent on things other than deciding, such as seeing the object and pulling the trigger. How it was fitted: hierarchical Bayesian estimation (pools officers, keeps each officer's own values); every parameter has an officer-level random intercept. Start point and threshold vary by race × behaviour; drift and NDT′ by race × object × behaviour. Model comparison put the scenario and suspect (actor) effects on the start point. "Credible" = 95% HDI excludes a region of practical equivalence (±0.05 for β and NDT′, ±0.1 for α and δ). Published results (Figure 5): - Start point (5A): no race effect (−0.005 [−0.037, 0.028]), so no pre-object bias to shoot Black suspects; officers start further from shoot with antagonistic suspects (−0.056). - Threshold (5B): lower for nonantagonistic Black than White suspects (−0.21), higher for antagonistic Black suspects (+0.48). - Drift (5C): race × object × behaviour interaction; for unarmed nonantagonistic suspects, evidence against shooting is stronger for White than Black suspects (−0.69 [−0.79, −0.59]); no credible race gap in the other three cells. - NDT′ (5D): higher for unarmed nonantagonistic Black suspects (+0.07), lower for antagonistic Black suspects (−0.10). The paper's reading: weaker drift plus a lower threshold for unarmed, nonantagonistic Black suspects explains their higher error rate; race acts as a cue to disambiguate threat when behaviour is ambiguous. Our version (slide 9): the same model plus st0 (trial-to-trial variability in non-decision time) and the previous trial on the start point; the visual information model then adds gun score and wrist speed on drift. IF ASKED: Shoot rate is each officer's own proportion in the cell, averaged over officers, with a 95 percent t interval across officers. Published drift difference: about −0.69 [−0.79, −0.59]. Machine learning on pre-onset video features

Methods

  • Features: 23,206 pre-onset summaries (mean, SD, slope, last value) of motion, image and audio embeddings, CLIP text probes, pose and object detections.
  • Models: all features enter 22 tree models (single trees, random forests, extra trees, gradient boosting); no pre-selection.
  • Test: leave-one-scenario-out, 8 folds; importance = mean |SHAP| on held-out videos.

What the top feature tracks: the right wrist moving toward the waist

Strongest links: the right wrist moving toward the waist (r = −0.41) and a calm, cooperative, nonantagonistic look (r = +0.40), across 64 videos.

SCRIPT: Before building the decision model, we asked a broader question with machine learning: is there information in the video before the object appears that predicts the outcome? [Click 1] We extracted 23,206 features from the lead-in of each video: motion, image and audio embeddings, text probes, pose and object detections. All of them went into 22 tree models with no pre-selection, and we tested on held-out scenarios, training on seven and testing on the eighth. [Click 2] Can these features tell whether a gun is coming? Somewhat. The model reaches an AUROC of 0.66, better than shuffled labels, but this is weak evidence. [Click 3] Can they predict the officer's decision? Here are the features the best model relied on. The top one is a motion feature from the last second before the object appears. [Click 4] When we unpack that feature, it tracks the right wrist moving toward the waist, and a calm, cooperative look: a waistband reach in an otherwise calm suspect. On the right is the wrist relationship on its own. Each point is a video. Moving left, the right wrist closes in on the waist over the lead-in, and the top feature goes up. The correlation is minus 0.41 across all 64 videos. So the takeaway is: before the object appears there is little reliable information. The decision is mostly driven by what happens after onset. That led us to the two measures in the next slides. SCRIPT: Now the model. Both models build on the published drift-diffusion model from Pleskac et al. 2025. In a drift-diffusion model, evidence starts at a start point, accumulates at a rate called the drift, and the officer responds when it reaches a threshold; non-decision time covers things like perception and pulling the trigger. Our base model adds two things to the published one: trial-to-trial variability in non-decision time, and the previous trial on the start point. [Click 1] The visual information model is identical except for one row: the drift. We add the gun score and the wrist speed to the drift. Each measure gets one slope per object and behaviour condition, shared by Black and White videos. So any change in the race gap has to come from the videos themselves, not from a separate race slope. IF ASKED: Previous trial: after a gun video officers start closer to shoot (slope +0.19). The non-decision-time variability fixes the too-slow predicted false alarms. 8 slopes total. Fitted in Stan, 3 chains of 1,000 draws.

Takeaways

Visual information improves the temporal decision model: adding gun-likeness and the wrist trend to drift gains +742 ELPD

It accounts for much of the race gap: the unarmed nonantagonistic drift gap falls from 0.68 to 0.27 at matched video features; smaller, but still above zero.

Next, eye-tracking: use where people actually look to validate these measures and learn which visual features they use to make that critical decision.
